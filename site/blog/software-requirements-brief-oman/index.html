How to Write a Software Requirements Brief That Gets Accurate Vendor Quotes

"Just build me something like Careem, but for laundry." Vendors hear this constantly, and the quote it produces is a guess wearing a number. A one-page brief, written before you contact anyone, is the single cheapest thing you can do to get accurate prices, realistic timelines, and a system that actually matches what your business needs.

Why "just build me an app" gets you bad quotes

When a client asks for a rough idea, a vendor has two choices: ask twenty clarifying questions before quoting (most won't bother, especially for a first conversation), or fill the gaps with assumptions and quote on that. Assumptions almost always favor the vendor's convenience, not your business — a generic user login instead of the approval workflow you actually run, a single branch instead of the five you operate, English-only instead of the bilingual interface your staff and customers need.

The result shows up later, not at signing. Change requests appear once development starts, the timeline slips, and the final price is nothing like the number you compared vendors on. None of this is dishonesty — it's the natural outcome of quoting against a vague scope. A clear brief removes the guesswork on both sides, which is also why serious vendors respond better to clients who bring one.

The eight things a requirements brief actually needs

This isn't a formal specification document — you don't need a business analyst or a template full of jargon. It's a plain-language brief that answers the questions a vendor needs to price your project properly.

SectionWhat to include
The problemOne paragraph, no technology terms: what's broken or missing today, and for whom.
Users and rolesWho uses the system, roughly how many of each, and what each role can and can't do.
Core workflowsThe 3–5 things the system must do on day one, written as step-by-step scenarios, not feature lists.
DataWhat information the system stores, where it comes from today (Excel, paper, another system), and who needs to see it.
IntegrationsEvery other system it must talk to — accounting software, a payment gateway, SMS, an existing CRM — named specifically.
Non-functional needsArabic and English support, expected number of concurrent users, uptime expectations, and any PDPL data-residency requirement.
ConstraintsA rough budget band, a real deadline (or "flexible"), and any technology the vendor must or must not use.
Out of scopeWhat you're deliberately not asking for in version one — this alone prevents most scope creep.

Notice what's missing from that list: screen designs, database schemas, and technical architecture. Those are the vendor's job to propose, not yours to dictate. Over-specifying the "how" at this stage narrows your options and often costs more, because you've designed around one way of solving the problem before a specialist has looked at it.

A brief you can actually write in a week

Most business owners stall here because "requirements document" sounds like a multi-week exercise. It doesn't have to be. A workable approach:

  1. Day 1–2: Write the problem paragraph and list every role who will touch the system. Don't overthink wording — clarity matters more than polish.
  2. Day 3: Walk through your current process step by step, on paper or in a spreadsheet, and mark where it breaks down today. This becomes your core workflows section almost automatically.
  3. Day 4: List every system you already use that this one needs to connect to. Check with whoever runs your accounting or POS software — you will forget at least one integration otherwise.
  4. Day 5: Add constraints — budget band, deadline, and anything non-negotiable (Arabic UI, on-premise hosting, a specific payment provider).
  5. Day 6–7: Have someone outside the project read it and try to picture the system from your description alone. If they can't, a vendor can't either.

That's it. Two to three pages is normal for a small business system; five is normal for something touching multiple departments. If it's growing past that, you're probably designing the solution rather than describing the problem — pull back.

How much detail is enough: MVP vs. full system

The right level of detail depends on what you're building first. Trying to fully specify a large system before writing a line of code is its own risk — requirements go stale, and you pay to document features that turn out to be unnecessary once real users are involved.

MVP / first versionFull system rollout
Core workflowsDetailed, step by stepDetailed, step by step
Secondary featuresNamed only, not detailedDetailed once the MVP is validated
IntegrationsOnly the ones you can't launch withoutFull integration map
Reporting/analyticsOne or two must-have reportsComplete reporting requirements

Mistakes that wreck quotes and timelines

  • Feature lists instead of workflows. "Needs a dashboard" tells a vendor nothing about what decisions that dashboard supports. Describe the scenario: "the branch manager needs to see today's unpaid invoices in under 5 seconds."
  • Forgetting Arabic from the start. Adding a second language after launch usually means redesigning screens, not just translating text. If staff or customers need Arabic, say so on page one.
  • No definition of "done." Without a clear line for what version one must do, both sides negotiate scope forever. Write it down, even if it changes later.
  • Silence on data migration. If you have existing records in Excel, another system, or on paper, say how many rows and how messy the data is. This is one of the most underestimated line items in any quote.
  • Hiding the budget. Vendors size a proposal to fit a budget range far more often than clients assume. Withholding it usually gets you a quote pitched at what the vendor thinks you can afford, not what your actual scope needs.

Sending it out: getting quotes you can actually compare

Send the same brief to every vendor on your short list, word for word — this is what makes the quotes comparable at all. Ask each one to return three things alongside their price: what they assumed where your brief was silent, what's explicitly excluded from their number, and a rough timeline broken into milestones rather than a single end date. A vendor who can't answer those three cleanly is telling you something about how the project will go once it starts. For the rest of the evaluation — references, contracts, red flags — our vendor evaluation checklist picks up exactly where this brief leaves off.

One brief, reused across every conversation, is also what lets you negotiate from facts instead of gut feel: when three vendors quote wildly different numbers against the identical scope, the difference tells you something real about their assumptions, their margins, or their capability — worth asking about directly before you sign anything.
