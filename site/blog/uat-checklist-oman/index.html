User Acceptance Testing: A Practical Checklist Before You Sign Off on New Software

The invoice lands. The vendor says the system is "ready to go live." And nobody outside the development team has actually tried to use it yet. Here is how to run a UAT process that catches real problems before your money and your reputation are on the line.

What UAT actually is, and why skipping it costs more later

User Acceptance Testing (UAT) is the stage where the people who will actually use the software — not the developers who built it — try it against real business scenarios and decide whether it does the job. It is different from the testing your vendor already does during development. Their testing checks that the code doesn't crash. Your testing checks that the software matches how your business actually works.

Many Oman businesses treat this stage as a formality: a quick click-through, a verbal "looks fine," and the final invoice gets paid. The problem shows up two weeks later, in production, when a sales invoice rounds incorrectly, an Arabic name gets mangled in a customer-facing report, or a warehouse clerk discovers there is no way to reverse a stock transfer. By then, the vendor's team has moved to the next client, and fixing it competes with new work instead of being covered under the original contract.

A proper UAT process is not bureaucracy. It is the last, cheapest point at which you can catch a mistake — before it is your customers, not your testers, who find it.

When to start planning UAT — not after development is "done"

The biggest timing mistake is treating UAT as something you think about once the vendor says they're finished. By then it's too late to plan properly, and every day of testing feels like it's delaying a launch everyone is already anxious about.

Plan UAT at the same time you write your requirements. If you used a structured brief when scoping the project — see our guide to writing a software requirements brief — every requirement in that document should become a test scenario later. That link is what makes UAT meaningful instead of random poking around: you are checking the system against what you asked for, not against what feels intuitively right on the day.

Put a UAT window into the contract and timeline from day one — typically two to four weeks for a mid-size business system, longer for anything touching finance, inventory, or multiple departments. Rushed UAT is barely better than none.

The four things your UAT plan must cover

1. A realistic test environment. Testing should happen on a system that mirrors production — same integrations, similar data volumes, real-looking records (not three sample rows). If your business runs bilingual documents, invoices, or customer communications, test with genuine Arabic and English content together, not placeholder text. Right-to-left layout bugs and Arabic text-encoding issues are some of the most common defects that slip through when testing is done only in English.

2. Scenarios that map to real work, not just the happy path. Every core business process needs a scenario: creating an order, cancelling one, issuing a refund, handling a customer with two invoices in different currencies, generating month-end reports. Then add the awkward cases that actually happen at work — a user without the right permission trying an action, a network drop mid-transaction, two staff editing the same record at once.

3. The right people doing the testing. Testing done only by the business owner, or only by the vendor's own QA team, misses different things. Bring in the actual staff who will use each module daily — the accountant, the warehouse supervisor, the front-desk clerk. They know the edge cases and the shortcuts that a generic test script never anticipates, and they are the ones who need to trust the system once it launches.

4. A written defect log, not a WhatsApp thread. Every issue found needs a written record: what happened, what should have happened, how to reproduce it, and a severity rating. Verbal feedback and scattered chat messages get lost, and without a paper trail there is no way to prove, later, that an issue was raised and never fixed.

Tying UAT sign-off to your final payment

This is the part contracts often get wrong: payment milestones that are tied to a delivery date instead of to actual acceptance. Build your contract so the final payment (commonly 10–20% of project value, held as retention) is released only after formal UAT sign-off, defined against clear severity levels:

SeverityWhat it meansRelease condition
BlockerCore process fails or data is corruptedZero allowed before go-live
CriticalA required feature doesn't work as specifiedZero allowed, or a written fix commitment with a date
MajorWorkaround exists but the process is painfulDocumented, fixed within an agreed warranty period
MinorCosmetic or low-impact issueLogged for a future update, not a blocker

Pair this with a short warranty period after go-live — 30 to 90 days is typical — during which the vendor fixes anything UAT missed at no extra cost. A practical UAT checklist

  • Every requirement from the original brief has at least one matching test scenario.
  • Test data includes real-looking Arabic and English content, not placeholders.
  • Actual end users from each department test their own workflows, not just IT.
  • Mobile and desktop views are both tested, if the system is used on both.
  • Permissions and roles are tested — including what a restricted user should not be able to do.
  • Integrations with existing systems (accounting, government portals, payment gateways) are tested end-to-end, not assumed to work because each side "should" be fine.
  • Every defect is logged in writing with severity, steps to reproduce, and an owner.
  • A sign-off document lists remaining known issues and who accepted them, and by when they'll be fixed.
  • The final payment and warranty period are explicitly linked to this sign-off in the contract.

Common mistakes to avoid

The same handful of mistakes show up across most projects that go wrong at this stage. Testing only the "happy path" and skipping error cases. Letting the vendor's team run UAT on your behalf, which tests the vendor's understanding of your business rather than your actual needs. Accepting a verbal promise that "we'll fix it after launch" in place of a written commitment. And the most costly one: paying the final invoice before sign-off, which removes any real leverage to get outstanding issues fixed.

A realistic timeline

For a typical mid-size business system — a CRM, an inventory platform, a customer portal — plan for one to two weeks of structured testing by real users, followed by a short fix-and-retest cycle of three to seven days for anything above "minor" severity. Larger systems touching finance or multiple locations need four or more weeks. Whatever the size, build the UAT window into your project timeline from the start rather than squeezing it in at the end — a UAT phase treated as an afterthought produces exactly the shallow testing that lets real problems through.
